# Evidence-based curiosity and narrative retention

Use curiosity to help the reader examine a real operating problem. Guide the
reader toward a useful conclusion without deception, coercion, or hidden
commercial pressure.

## Research translated into writing rules

### Create a specific information gap

Loewenstein's information-gap theory describes curiosity as the tension created
when people notice a gap between what they know and what they want to know.

Writing rule:

- Name a familiar situation.
- Surface one missing explanation that matters to the reader.
- Make the gap specific enough that the reader can predict the value of the
  answer.
- Resolve the gap inside the piece. Never promise an answer that the content
  does not deliver.

Source: George Loewenstein, *The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and
Reinterpretation*, Psychological Bulletin 116(1), 75-98 (1994).
https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75

### Ask for a prediction before revealing the mechanism

Kang and colleagues found that stronger epistemic curiosity was associated with
greater willingness to seek an answer and better later recall of surprising
answers.

Writing rule:

- Present a concrete question whose intuitive answer appears obvious.
- Let the reader form that answer.
- Introduce one credible contradiction quickly.
- Explain the mechanism and practical solution.

Source: Kang et al., *The Wick in the Candle of Learning*, Psychological
Science 20(8), 963-973 (2009).
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x

### Build transportation with a concrete operating scene

Green and Brock found that narrative transportation combines focused attention,
imagery, and emotion and can strengthen story-consistent beliefs.

Writing rule:

- Open in one recognisable moment: a quote waiting in an inbox, two reports
  disagreeing, or an order failing between Salesforce and the ERP.
- Use concrete actions and objects instead of abstract claims.
- Keep one point of view and one unresolved operating question.
- Move from scene to explanation, then from explanation to action.

Source: Green and Brock, *The Role of Transportation in the Persuasiveness of
Public Narratives*, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 79(5),
701-721 (2000).
https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701

### Use activation carefully

Berger and Milkman found that high-arousal emotion was associated with greater
sharing, while practical utility and interest also contributed.

Writing rule:

- Prefer recognition, surprise, controlled concern, and practical usefulness.
- Make the operational consequence concrete.
- Do not manufacture anger, anxiety, urgency, or fear.
- End with agency: a decision, diagnostic question, or next action the reader
  can take.

Source: Berger and Milkman, *What Makes Online Content Viral?*, Journal of
Marketing Research 49(2), 192-205 (2012).
https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353

### Keep headlines informative

Question headlines have mixed evidence. Large-scale research reported in 2026
found that question-framed titles reduced engagement because readers perceived
them as less informative. Other research found that curiosity gaps work best
at a middle level of concreteness and can backfire when they are too vague.

Writing rule:

- Do not force a question mark into every title.
- State the subject and consequence while leaving one meaningful mechanism
  unresolved.
- Prefer an implicit question over an empty teaser.
- Make the first paragraph clarify exactly what the reader will learn.

Sources:

- Fang and Wheeler, *Titles Framed as Questions Reduce Reader Engagement*,
  Journal of Consumer Psychology (2026).
  https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/titles-framed-questions-reduce-reader-engagement
- *When Curiosity Gaps Backfire: Effects of Headline Concreteness on
  Information Selection Decisions*.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11704130/

### Protect trust

Experiments on clickbait found that manipulative headlines can reduce perceived
credibility. Research on clickbait sharing also found that perceived
manipulation can lower trust and willingness to share.

Writing rule:

- The headline, opening, and conclusion must describe the same problem.
- Reveal the core answer early enough to respect the reader's time.
- Never hide essential caveats or exaggerate the stakes.
- Never fabricate evidence, certainty, client stories, or results.
- Persuasion must come from recognition, reasoning, and useful proof.

Sources:

- *Clickbait: Trust and Credibility of Digital News* (2021).
  https://doi.org/10.1109/TTS.2021.3073464
- *Did Clickbait Crack the Code on Virality?*
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8799425/

## Curiosity-to-solution sequence

Use this sequence for blog posts and adapt it to shorter social posts:

1. **Familiar scene:** Show one concrete operating moment.
2. **Apparent answer:** State or invite the explanation most leaders would
   initially give.
3. **Contradiction:** Present one verified detail that makes that answer
   incomplete.
4. **Guiding question:** Name the exact question the piece will resolve.
5. **Mechanism:** Explain why the problem behaves this way.
6. **Solution:** Give a practical framework, decision, or diagnostic.
7. **Payoff:** Close the original question explicitly.
8. **Next step:** Offer one calm action or conversation.

## Retention checks

- The title identifies the subject and consequence.
- The first 100 words establish one useful information gap.
- Each section either deepens or closes that gap.
- The answer begins before reader patience becomes the cost.
- The conclusion explicitly resolves the opening question.
- The reader receives practical value without contacting Osmel.
- The CTA follows value and uses a conversation verb.
- No headline or sentence depends on deception, panic, or withheld context.
