The Approval Queue That Stops Orders

An approval workflow can look harmless because nothing is technically broken. The quote exists. The ERP is available. The customer is waiting. The problem is that nobody can see whose turn it is.

A confirmed quote is ready on Monday morning. Sales believes finance has to approve the margin. Finance is waiting for operations to confirm availability. Operations thinks the ERP already blocked the order because one field is missing. By Thursday, the customer has asked for an update and everyone is searching through inboxes.

The obvious answer is to automate the approval. Add a button. Send a notification. Move the request faster.

That answer is often incomplete. Many approval problems are not slow because the company lacks automation. They are slow because the approval queue is invisible, ownership is unclear, and exceptions have no agreed path.

The queue is the real process

Leaders usually describe the official process: a quote above a threshold needs approval, a discount needs review, a delivery exception needs confirmation, or a new customer needs a credit check. On paper, that is clear.

The real process is the queue. Where does the request wait? Who can see it? Who owns it when the normal approver is away? What happens when the request is incomplete? How does the business know whether it is waiting for a decision, a data correction, or a system error?

If those questions are not answered, the approval does not live in Salesforce, the ERP, or an automation platform. It lives in messages, memory, and follow-up habits. That is why the process feels personal. The company depends on someone remembering to ask again.

The practical test: Pick one approval from last week. Can you see when it entered the queue, who owned it, why it waited, and what decision moved it forward? If not, the queue is hidden.

Why automation can make it worse

Automation is useful when the rule is clear. It is risky when the rule is assumed. A faster notification does not decide who owns an exception. A reminder email does not explain whether finance, operations, or sales has the next action. A status field does not help if nobody agrees what the status means.

When a hidden queue is automated too early, the company often gets more movement without more control. Requests are routed, copied, escalated, and closed, but leaders still cannot answer the operational question: what is stuck and why?

This is where approval workflows become management work, not only IT work. The system can carry the process, but the business must define the decision.

What a visible approval workflow needs

A reliable approval workflow does not need to be complex. It needs a few decisions that are explicit enough for both people and software.

1. One queue per decision type

A margin approval, a credit check, and a delivery exception may all relate to the same order. They should not become one vague waiting status. Each decision type needs its own owner, trigger, and exit condition.

2. Clear ownership and substitutes

The workflow should know who owns the decision and who acts when that person is unavailable. If the answer is an inbox alias with no owner, the queue will still depend on memory.

3. A visible reason for waiting

Waiting for approval is not enough. The reason matters. Waiting for margin review is different from waiting for missing product data or a blocked customer record. The reason tells the next person what to fix.

4. Escalation that respects the business

Escalation should not be noise. It should happen when a decision has waited longer than the business can accept, or when a customer promise is at risk. The rule should be known before the queue is full.

5. A record of the decision

The company should be able to see who approved, what was approved, and which data was used. This protects the process when questions appear later. It also shows whether the rule is working.

Where to look first

Start with one approval that regularly delays orders, quotes, or fulfilment. Do not begin with the whole company. Follow the request from the moment it is created until the decision is recorded.

Ask five simple questions:

  1. What event creates the approval? Name the field, threshold, status, or exception that starts the queue.
  2. Where does the request wait? Find the system, inbox, list, spreadsheet, or person carrying it.
  3. Who owns the next decision? Separate decision ownership from technical ownership.
  4. What information is missing when it waits? Many approvals are blocked by incomplete data, not disagreement.
  5. How does the business see delay? If delay is only visible after a customer asks, the workflow is not visible enough.

The useful outcome

The goal is not to make every approval instant. Some approvals should take time because they protect margin, delivery quality, or customer risk.

The goal is to remove unnecessary waiting. A good workflow shows the queue, names the owner, explains the reason for delay, and records the decision. Once that is clear, automation has something solid to carry.

Before adding another approval tool, inspect the queue you already have. If you cannot see whose turn it is, the first improvement is visibility, not speed.
