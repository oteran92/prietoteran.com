When Approval Workflows Become Hidden Queues

A digital approval flow can still behave like an inbox. The request exists, but nobody can say who owns the pause, what happens next, or when the business should intervene.

A purchase request leaves the ERP at 09:14. The operations manager expects a decision before lunch. At 16:30 the status still says pending. The request has not failed. It has not been rejected. It is simply waiting somewhere nobody can see clearly.

The first explanation is usually simple: the approver is busy. That may be true. But it is rarely the whole problem. In many B2B companies, approvals stall because the workflow has been digitized without making ownership visible.

The form moved from paper or email into a tool. The queue did not become easier to control.

The workflow exists, but the pause has no owner

Approval workflows are meant to create control. A request is submitted, checked, approved, rejected, or returned with a question. In theory, every step is clear.

In practice, the unclear part is often the pause between steps. Who notices that a request has waited too long? Who decides whether it should be escalated? Who is allowed to approve when the usual approver is travelling? Who records why an exception was accepted?

If those questions are not answered, the workflow depends on memory and personal follow-up. Someone sends a message. Someone forwards an email. Someone walks across the office. The system may show a status, but the real process has left the system.

That is how a digital workflow becomes a hidden queue.

Why adding automation too early makes it worse

It is tempting to respond with more automation. Send reminders. Add escalation emails. Trigger a Teams message after two days. These can help, but only after the decision model is clear.

If the process does not define who owns the pause, reminders create noise. If the exception path is unclear, escalation creates conflict. If the source data is weak, automatic approval rules move bad requests faster.

Automation does not create accountability. It repeats the accountability model you give it. When that model is missing, automation repeats confusion at higher speed.

The practical test: Pick one approval that was late last month. Can you see who had it, how long it waited, why it paused, and what rule decided the next step? If not, you do not have an automation problem first. You have a visibility problem.

Three things to make visible before you automate

A reliable approval workflow does not need to be complex. It needs to make three operating facts visible.

1. The queue

Every pending approval should be visible in one place, with age, owner, and business impact. A request waiting for a CHF 800 office item and a request blocking a customer shipment should not look identical. The queue should help the business decide where attention belongs.

2. The owner

Every pause needs a named owner. Not a department. Not a role in theory. A person or accountable function that knows the request is waiting and knows what action is expected. Ownership can rotate, but it cannot be vague.

3. The exception path

Most approval workflows fail at the exception, not at the standard case. A price override, an urgent shipment, a missing cost center, a customer promise already made. The workflow needs to say what happens when the request does not fit the normal rule.

Without an exception path, people solve the case outside the system. Later, nobody can explain why the decision was made, only that work had to continue.

A better sequence

Start with one approval flow that slows operations every week. Map it from request to decision. Do not begin with software. Begin with the moments where work stops.

  1. Name the decision. What is being approved, and what risk is the approval meant to control?
  2. Name the required data. Which fields must be correct before someone can decide?
  3. Name the owner of each pause. Who acts, who covers, and who escalates?
  4. Name the timeout. When does waiting become a business issue?
  5. Name the exception path. What happens when the normal approver or normal rule is not enough?

Only then decide what the system should do automatically. Some steps may become simple rules. Some may only need better visibility. Some should remain human decisions, but with better context and a clear record.

The payoff is not speed alone

Faster approvals are useful, but they are not the main benefit. The real benefit is control. Leaders can see where requests wait, why they wait, and which rules create friction. Operations can stop chasing messages. Finance can see the decision trail. Teams can improve the process because the process is visible.

This is also where AI becomes safer later. An AI assistant can summarize requests, check missing fields, or route standard approvals only when the underlying ownership model is already clear. Otherwise it becomes another layer on top of an invisible queue.

If approvals are stuck in your company, do not ask first which tool can automate them. Ask where the pause lives, who owns it, and whether the system can prove both. That is where operational control begins.
