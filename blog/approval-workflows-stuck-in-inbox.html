Why Approval Workflows Still Get Stuck

An approval workflow can look finished in the system and still depend on one person noticing an email. That is where the real bottleneck hides.

A quote is ready. Sales has entered the details in Salesforce. The ERP has enough information to reserve stock. Finance only needs to approve a margin exception before the order can move.

The workflow says pending approval. The real process says something else: an email is waiting in an inbox, mixed with customer replies, internal questions, and meeting notes.

When leaders see this delay, the apparent answer is simple. Send more reminders. Add another notification. Ask managers to respond faster. But the uncomfortable detail is this: if the decision still lives in email, the workflow has not been designed. It has only been named.

The system records the state. The inbox controls the pace.

Many approval processes are half digital. The request starts in a CRM, ERP, or ticketing system. The decision then leaves the system as an email. Someone replies, forwards, asks for context, or waits until the end of the day. Later, someone updates the original record.

On paper, the process has an approval step. In practice, the company has a hidden queue. Nobody can reliably see who has the decision, why it is waiting, what information is missing, or what happens if the approver is away.

This matters because approvals usually sit at points where the business wants control. Price exceptions. Credit checks. Delivery commitments. Purchase requests. Customer onboarding. These are not administrative details. They decide whether work moves or stops.

Why reminders do not fix the workflow

A reminder can help when the process is clear and someone forgot. It does not help when the process itself is unclear.

Before adding more alerts, look for three common gaps.

1. The owner is unclear

Some approvals depend on a role, but the system sends the request to a person. When that person is travelling, unavailable, or no longer responsible for that customer, the work waits. The system needs an ownership rule, not only an email address.

2. The threshold is unclear

Teams often ask for approval because they are unsure whether approval is needed. A margin exception, discount level, delivery promise, or credit condition should have a visible rule. If the rule is hidden in habit, every exception becomes a discussion.

3. The next step is unclear

Approved is not enough. The workflow should know what changes after approval: which field updates, which team is notified, which document is released, which order status changes, and which exception remains open.

The practical test: Choose one approval that delayed work last week. Can you see who owned it, why it waited, what data was missing, and what changed after approval? If not, the inbox is still part of your system architecture.

What a controlled approval workflow needs

A good approval workflow does not have to be complex. It has to keep the decision inside a visible operating model.

For Swiss and DACH B2B companies, that usually means five simple design choices.

1. A named business object

The approval should belong to something concrete: a quote, order, customer, invoice, purchase request, or delivery promise. If the approval only exists as an email thread, it will be hard to audit and hard to improve.

2. A visible status model

Pending, approved, rejected, returned for clarification, and expired should not be vague labels. Each status should tell the next person what can happen now and what cannot happen yet.

3. Ownership by role and rule

The system should route the decision based on the account, amount, margin, risk, location, product line, or customer type. A person can still make the decision, but the assignment should not depend on somebody remembering who usually handles it.

4. Exception handling

Every approval flow needs a path for missing information, no response, substitute approvers, and rejected requests. Without exception handling, the company recreates the inbox as soon as something unusual happens.

5. A record of the decision

The business should be able to see who approved what, when, based on which data, and what changed afterward. This is not only about compliance. It is about learning which approvals are necessary and which ones have become habit.

How to start without overbuilding

Do not start with every approval in the company. Start with one handoff that delays revenue, delivery, or month-end work often enough that people have built workarounds around it.

Map the current path in plain language. What starts the request? Which system holds the data? Who decides? What information do they need? What happens if they approve, reject, or do nothing? Where does someone open Excel or send an email because the system cannot answer?

Then decide what should stay human and what should become system logic. The goal is not to remove judgment. The goal is to stop hiding judgment inside inboxes.

The useful question is not whether your company has an approval workflow. The useful question is whether the workflow still works when the inbox is removed. If it does not, that is the first place to fix.
