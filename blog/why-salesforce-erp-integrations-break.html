Salesforce ERP Sync Issues: Causes and Fixes

Missing orders, repeated errors or changes after an update: how to investigate where a Salesforce–ERP workflow breaks.

An order is confirmed in Salesforce but missing from your ERP. Before rebuilding the integration, trace that record: where was it last accepted, what response came back, and who can recover it?

This guide is a diagnostic starting point. The scenarios below illustrate failure modes; they are not client case studies or measured outcomes.

Three failure modes to investigate

Start by checking which of these symptoms matches the affected records:

  • Silent failures: Data stops syncing, but nobody notices until a customer calls about a missing order
  • Cascading errors: One failed transaction blocks everything behind it, creating a backlog of 100+ failed syncs
  • The vendor update: Your ERP provider pushes an update that changes a field name, and suddenly nothing works

A temporary patch may restore the flow without explaining why it failed. Keep the original error and record identifiers so the underlying cause can be investigated.

Your operations team adapts by building "backup processes" — spreadsheets, manual checks, weekly reconciliation meetings. The integration becomes the problem it was supposed to solve.

Why ERP Sync Issues Keep Coming Back (It's Not the Tools)

The issue isn't Salesforce. It's not your ERP. The middleware platform you chose is probably fine.

One place to investigate is architecture: three assumptions that look reasonable during the sales process but break under real-world conditions:

Assumption 1: "Real-time sync is always better"

Real-time synchronization sounds ideal. Quote created in Salesforce? Instantly appears in ERP. Perfect, right?

Until your ERP is down for 15 minutes during a planned maintenance window. Or a network hiccup causes a timeout. Or someone saves an incomplete record. Real-time sync means real-time failures.

Illustrative scenario: the ERP becomes unavailable during maintenance. Without durable storage and a recovery path, an order may remain missing after the ERP comes back online.

Assumption 2: "The integration handles everything automatically"

Most integrations are built for the happy path. Customer exists in both systems? Great. Standard product? No problem. Price list matches? Perfect.

But what happens when:

  • A new customer exists in Salesforce but not yet in ERP?
  • A special pricing agreement applies to this quote?
  • The product is available but needs a custom configuration?

Without explicit error handling for edge cases, the integration silently fails or, worse, creates bad data. You discover the problem when accounting reconciles the month and finds discrepancies.

Assumption 3: "Once it works, it keeps working"

Integration projects have a testing phase. Everything passes. You go live. Success.

Then your ERP vendor updates to version 12.3. A field name changes. A data type shifts from string to integer. An API endpoint gets deprecated.

The integration breaks. And because nobody documented which fields, endpoints, or transformations the integration actually uses, fixing it takes days of investigation.

Salesforce Architecture: Integration Patterns

How to Fix a Broken Salesforce Integration: The Architecture That Works

These architecture choices can make failures easier to recover from:

Principle 1: Design for Failure

Assume failures will happen. Build for them explicitly:

  • Choose durable queuing when the workflow permits asynchronous processing: When a record needs to sync, put it in a queue. Process the queue with retry logic. If something fails, it doesn't block everything else.
  • Implement exponential backoff: Increase the delay between retries, cap the number of attempts and respect API limits. Make repeated requests safe against duplicates.
  • Alert on patterns, not individual failures: One failed sync? Normal. Ten failures in an hour? Alert someone.

Retries help with temporary interruptions. Missing customer records, invalid values and permission errors need a different response: correct the cause before replaying the request.

Principle 2: Make Failures Visible

The worst failures are silent. Design for visibility:

  • Error dashboards: Show what failed, why, and what needs attention
  • Weekly summary reports: Track successful records, retries and records awaiting manual review separately.
  • Clear ownership: Someone needs to own integration health (usually operations, not IT)

You can't fix problems you don't know exist. Visibility is not optional.

Principle 3: Document Dependencies

When your integration breaks (and it will), you need to know:

  • Which API endpoints it uses
  • Which fields are mandatory vs optional
  • What data transformations happen
  • Which system is the source of truth for each data type

This documentation isn't for your current self. It's for your team six months from now when something breaks at 4 PM on Friday and you need to understand what changed.

Reality check: Good architecture won't prevent all failures. It makes failures manageable, visible, and fixable without emergency vendor support calls.

How to investigate a broken Salesforce integration

  1. Choose one affected order and record its ID in both systems. Compare it with a successful order.
  2. Find the last successful handoff. Check timestamps, response codes and integration logs.
  3. Check authentication, required fields, data mappings and recent API or configuration changes.
  4. Before replaying, verify whether the destination already created the order. Use a stable external ID to avoid duplicates.
  5. Test the correction on a controlled sample, then reconcile the affected records and document the recovery.

Agree who owns each field, which delays are acceptable and who handles records that cannot recover automatically. The right design depends on the workflow.

Questions to Ask Your Integration Vendor

Before your next integration project (or when evaluating your current one), ask:

  1. What happens when the ERP is temporarily unavailable? If the answer is "the sync will fail," ask about retry mechanisms.
  2. How do we know when something breaks? If the answer is "you'll see errors," ask for monitoring and alerting details.
  3. Who owns the documentation? If the answer is unclear or "we provide documentation," make sure you actually get usable documentation, not just API specs.
  4. What's the disaster recovery process? If something breaks at 6 PM on Friday, what happens? Is there a queue we can replay? Or do we lose data?
  5. Can our team modify this ourselves? Vendor lock-in isn't about the software. It's about knowledge lock-in.
